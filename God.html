I have structured the study in two parts. The first establishes what is meant by the sovereignty of God, and how this attribute of the Creator fits into the context of His character. The second explores some of the issues and implications of God's sovereignty, such as how it relates to human free will, man's moral responsibility for sin, and the dual problems of suffering and evil.

Part 1

Definitions and Distinctions

Wayne Grudem's Systematic Theology identifies three of God's attributes as "attributes of purpose."

God's Will "is that attribute of God whereby he approves and determines to bring about every action necessary for the existence and activity of himself and all creation." When it comes to God's will, though, theologians distinguish between God's "secret will" and his "revealed will." His secret will or "will of decree" is His decree concerning what will certainly occur. This is the one that pertains most to His "plan" for His creation. God's revealed will or "will of precept" means "His commands for mankind to obey and His declaration to us of what is pleasing in His sight." This is the one that pertains most to His "intention" for His creation. Note that this distinction roughly corresponds to what Pastor CJ referred to as the "general" and "specific" will of God in his recent sermon, "What Are You Doing Here?" (June 22, 2025). We will discuss more about the apparent difficulties of God's "plan" not always matching His "intention" later.

God's Freedom "is that attribute of God whereby he does whatever he pleases." He is not constrained by anything outside Himself.

God's Omnipotence "means that God is able to do all his holy will." Each of these distinct attributes represents one aspect of what we will refer to as God's sovereignty. In other words, His actions are chosen exclusively by Him, nothing and no one can stop them, and He has in Himself the power to accomplish them all.

Another meaningful distinction is that between sovereignty and providence. John Piper, in his book Providence, distinguishes the two by writing, "Sovereignty focuses on God’s right and power to do all that he wills, but in itself, it does not express any design or goal," whereas providence connects His right and power with His good and wise purpose. In other words, providence is "God's purposeful sovereignty." Piper notes that many traditional confessions of faith have held to this definition. For example, Question 18 of the Westminster Larger Catechism (1648) asks, "What are the works of providence?" and declares the following answer: "God’s works of providence are his most holy, wise, and powerful preserving and governing all his creatures; ordering them, and all their actions, to his own glory." In this study, we will tend use both terms interchangeably, such that God's sovereignty will almost always be paired with His purpose.

Creeds and Catechisms

From its earliest days, the Church has recognized God's absolute sovereignty. The Nicene Creed, for example, begins, "I believe in one God, the Father almighty, maker of heaven and earth, of all things visible and invisible." The term "almighty" here rightly conveys God's sovereignty over His creation, and has been thus translated (by Piper and others) as "all-sovereign." (see, e.g. page 24 of Piper's The Pleasures of God, available here).

The Catechism of the Catholic Church (303) "The witness of Scripture is unanimous that the solicitude of divine providence is concrete and immediate; God cares for all, from the least things to the great events of the world and its history. The sacred books powerfully affirm God's absolute sovereignty over the course of events." (306) "God is the sovereign master of his plan. But to carry it out he also makes use of his creatures' cooperation. This use is not a sign of weakness, but rather a token of almighty God's greatness and goodness. For God grants his creatures not only their existence, but also the dignity of acting on their own, of being causes and principles for each other, and thus of cooperating in the accomplishment of his plan." (308) "The truth that God is at work in all the actions of his creatures is inseparable from faith in God the Creator. God is the first cause, who operates in and through secondary causes." Reference is here made to Philippians 2:13, which I also cite below.

Numerous catechisms and confessions of faith coming out of the Reformation aver the same conception of God's sovereignty, as pointed out in Piper's Providence. The Belgic Confession of 1561, Article 13, The Doctrine of God’s Providence, states, "We believe that this good God, after creating all things, did not abandon them to chance or fortune but leads and governs them according to his holy will, in such a way that nothing happens in this world without God’s orderly arrangement." The Westminster Larger Catechism referenced above speaks of God's providence similarly. And the Westminster Confession of Faith of 1646, Chapter 5, Of Providence, 5.1 states, "God the great Creator of all things doth uphold, direct, dispose, and govern all creatures, actions, and things, from the greatest even to the least, by his most wise and holy providence, according to his infallible foreknowledge, and the free and immutable counsel of his own will, to the praise of the glory of his wisdom, power, justice, goodness, and mercy."

What I want us to see is from these passages is that the Church has continually affirmed God's absolute sovereignty precisely as I have attempted to define it here. We must also not miss the fact that these sources are the work of devout and godly men who studied the Scriptures with greater intensity and intentionality than any of us on his best day. I believe, therefore, they are at least worthy of our consideration, if not our trust. I also want to point out that this definition is one of the many agreements between Catholic and Protestant doctrine.

Evil and Suffering

If God is absolutely sovereign, then why does He allow tragedies, atrocities, evil, and so forth? Two related questions are embedded here. The first, in its traditional framing (going all the way back to 300 BC, possibly due to the ancient Greek philosopher Epicurus), is known as "the problem of evil." How can an all-good, all-powerful God allow rape, cruelty, and genocide? Why doesn't he topple dictators or stop serial killers and terrorists? The traditional statement (thanks, Wikipedia) goes like this:
Is God willing to prevent evil, but not able? Then he is impotent.
Is he able, but not willing? then is he malevolent.
Is he both able and willing? Then evil must not exist.
Yet evil does exist, and so an all-powerful, all-good God must not.

Wikipedia points out that a similar idea is suggested even earlier in Buddhist documents that state, "if a God created sentient beings, then due to the pain and suffering they feel, he is likely to be an evil God." Both statements pose a similar conundrum to that which most humans have experienced at one point or another. How can God be God and yet evil persist in the world?

The second embedded question is that of suffering. Independent of evil, humanity nevertheless experiences pain, death, and tragedy. In this case the suffering is not at the hands of other human beings, but nature. The way I've heard this issue framed is, "How can God permit innocent people to suffer?" The terrible tragedies cataloged by Relief Web, for example?

I really appreciate the Catholic Catechism's take on this matter, when it references the writings of St. Augustine: (311) "For Almighty God ..., because he is supremely good, would never allow any evil whatsoever to exist in his works if he were not so all-powerful and good as to cause good to emerge from evil itself."
